Turn a reference video into a production shot list

To turn video into a shot list, separate what happens from where the edit changes. A timestamped report is a starting draft. Verify the source, group related action notes, then decide which camera setups your next production needs.

Published September 20, 2026 · Updated October 6, 2026 · 9 min read

Start with the edit, not the equipment

Break the reference into shots before choosing lenses or lighting. Mark every visible cut, then record each shot's duration. This reveals the pacing you need to reproduce and prevents a common mistake: matching one beautiful frame while missing the rhythm that makes the sequence work.

Here is an illustrative production plan, not output from an analyzed video:

00:00 - 00:02
Wide establishing shot

Subject enters frame left. Locked camera, cool daylight, location sound under music.

00:02 - 00:04
Medium product reveal

Slow push-in, shallow depth of field, product centered in the lower third.

00:04 - 00:06
Close detail

Hand interaction, hard side light, quick cut on movement.

An action timeline is not the same as a shot list

A character can lift an umbrella, move it, and let go while the camera view continues. Those are useful action notes, but each new action does not automatically require a new shot number. Keep three things separate: report ranges that help you locate a moment, verified edit boundaries in the reference, and planned camera setups for your own shoot.

Adobe's shot-list guide treats camera angle, movement, scene description and audio as separate planning fields. StudioBinder's shot-list documentation likewise organizes visual references by scenes, shots and setups. A long list of observations is not a substitute for deciding what coverage your crew needs.

Real example: four action ranges in an umbrella animation

This 25-second, founder-owned KAMOD animation was previously analyzed in AdScanVideo. The following entries condense the first 15 seconds of the existing report supplied by the founder. We have not run a new analysis or changed the ranges to make them look more precise.

Existing report rangeCondensed observationReplay with context
00:00 - 00:03A small blue knitted figure holds a yellow umbrella beside a flower.
00:03 - 00:06The figure moves the umbrella over the flower and plants its handle.
00:06 - 00:13The figure releases the umbrella and shivers in the rain.
00:13 - 00:15A large leaf covers the figure.

Replay buttons add up to half a second of context on each side. These are review aids, not exact cut markers.

The useful review question: did the shot change, or did the subject simply do something new? The report describes continuing full-shot framing across these action ranges. Treat that as an observation to check, not a certified cut count. Keep the umbrella actions together in a review note until you have inspected the edit. At about 15 seconds, the visual setting changes to a person at a desk. Inspect the transition in your editor before assigning a precise boundary.

A human review draft, not another AI result

Reference section: approximately 00:00 to 00:15. Action notes: hold umbrella, shelter flower, shiver, leaf provides cover. Framing to check: whole figure and flower remain visible. Cut boundaries: not frame-verified here. Audio: not verified in this silent example.

This grouping is a manual way to make the report usable. It does not claim AdScanVideo automatically merges entries or detects every cut.

Free video-to-shot-list worksheet

Download the blank CSV worksheet and open it in Excel, Google Sheets or your editing notes. It is an empty planning template, not an export of a paid analysis.

  1. Paste a report range into Reference range; leave verified cut times blank.
  2. Replay the range with a small amount of context. Check the requested action, visible text and, in your own source, relevant audio.
  3. Record a cut only after confirming it in the editor. Leave uncertain times marked Not verified.
  4. Combine repeated action descriptions when doing so preserves a meaningful change. Do not merge across a cut you confirmed.
  5. Choose the camera setup your new production needs. Mark lens, lighting and timing as creative decisions unless you have evidence for them.
  6. Assign an owner and a review status so the crew knows which rows are ready to use.

If your task is finding an occurrence rather than planning a shoot, use Find moments with a specific action and matching dialogue, then verify the candidate. Do not turn every returned moment into a separate production shot. See our guide to AI video analysis and frame-by-frame review for the limits of timestamped first drafts.

Record six decisions for every shot

  1. Time and duration: where the shot begins, ends, and how long it holds.
  2. Subject and action: who or what appears and what changes during the shot.
  3. Framing: wide, medium, close, insert, overhead, or another specific composition.
  4. Camera behavior: locked, handheld, pan, tilt, tracking move, push-in, or pull-out.
  5. Light and color: direction, contrast, time of day, palette, and practical sources.
  6. Audio: dialogue, sound cue, music beat, or silence that motivates the cut.

Convert observations into production instructions

A breakdown describes what the reference contains. A production shot list adds what your crew needs to recreate it. Translate “slow push toward a coffee cup” into a track length, lens range, approximate focus distance, prop position, and the action that starts the move. Keep uncertain details marked as choices rather than pretending the reference proves them.

Shot-list columns worth keeping

  • Shot number and reference timestamp
  • Framing and camera movement
  • Subject, action, and dialogue
  • Location, props, wardrobe, and talent
  • Lighting direction and time-of-day requirement
  • Estimated setup time and production priority

Where AI helps and where the crew decides

Video analysis can produce timestamped observations about visible action, framing and camera movement, plus dialogue when usable audio is available. It may repeat actions, miss transitions or infer details incorrectly. It is not guaranteed to enumerate every shot. It cannot know the original lens, exact light output, budget, location restrictions, or safety requirements from pixels alone. A director or cinematographer should convert the observations into final technical choices.

Common questions

Does every timestamped entry mean a new shot?

No. Entries can describe different actions within continuing framing. Verify actual cuts in the source before numbering shots.

Can I use the report timestamps as exact edit points?

Use them to find a candidate range. Confirm the boundary in your editor before making an exact cut or calculating frame-level duration.

Does AI know the original lens or light setup?

A visible look can suggest an approach, but the report does not prove focal length, light output or the original equipment. Record those as choices or uncertainties.
