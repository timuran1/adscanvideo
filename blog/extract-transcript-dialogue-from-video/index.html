Choose the right source for the words

Dialogue can come from embedded subtitles, platform captions, or speech recognition applied to the audio track. These sources have different strengths. Captions are fast and often clean, but they may omit pauses, speaker names, or words the publisher considered unimportant. Speech recognition can recover dialogue from an uploaded file, but accents, overlapping voices, music, and poor microphones reduce accuracy.

If the video has no usable captions or audio transcript, describe it as visual-only. Invented dialogue is worse than a clearly stated limitation.

A transcript workflow that produces usable notes

1. Confirm the file and audio
Play several points in the video. Check that speech is audible and belongs to the visible footage.
2. Preserve timestamps
Keep each statement attached to a time range so an editor or reviewer can return to the source.
3. Separate speakers carefully
Use names only when the video establishes them. Otherwise use stable visual labels such as “speaker in blue jacket.”
4. Join words to scenes
Record what is on screen while the dialogue occurs. This matters when a speaker says “this,” points at an object, or reacts to an edit.
5. Verify important quotes
Replay names, numbers, claims, and short quotable lines before publishing or relying on them.

Transcript, subtitles, and dialogue notes are different outputs

  • Transcript: a chronological record of spoken words.
  • Subtitles: short, readable text timed for on-screen display.
  • Dialogue notes: the spoken content connected to speakers, shots, actions, and decisions.

For editing, research, and ad analysis, dialogue notes are often the most useful because they preserve why a line matters. For accessibility or publication, you may still need a separate subtitle file and a human review pass.

Verify a selected quote without scrubbing the whole recording

For an edit brief, start with a shortlist of the lines you intend to use. This narrows the review task; it does not certify the rest of the transcript. If you are publishing a complete transcript, review the complete recording.

  1. Save the candidate. Copy its generated wording, reported timestamp, and intended use into the worksheet. Mark it Unverified.
  2. Replay with context. In the original player, start a few seconds before the reported range and listen beyond its end. If the line is absent, expand the window and record the corrected range rather than treating the report's time as exact.
  3. Check the words that change meaning. Listen for names, quantities, negation, units, and qualifiers such as “may” or “up to.” Do not silently turn a possibility into a promise.
  4. Check who said it and what it refers to. Identify speaker changes and the preceding question. Record relevant screen text or demonstrations separately from speech.
  5. Choose the outcome. Mark Verified verbatim, Corrected after replay, Paraphrase only, or Omit: unclear. Save the corrected wording and the range you actually checked.

If two similar actions occur, visual similarity alone does not prove which occurrence contains the dialogue. Check both candidate ranges and keep the one that contains the audible line and the intended action together. If neither does, record no verified match.

Keep quotes, paraphrases, and scene observations separate

  • Verbatim quote: wording checked against the audio, with attribution and source range.
  • Paraphrase: your condensed explanation of the speaker's point, without quotation marks and without adding an unsupported claim.
  • Scene observation: what the image shows, such as a speaker pointing at a diagram. It cannot establish what was said.

YouTube's automatic-caption guidance warns that pronunciation, accents, dialects, and background noise can change the recognized speech, and asks creators to review the output. This supports checking the source instead of trusting polished wording alone.

W3C's transcript guidance distinguishes speech and meaningful non-speech audio from visual information added to a descriptive transcript. Keep those evidence types clear. For a timed caption track, use our caption quality checklist and a caption editor; a video analysis report is not an SRT file.

Common questions

Can I use a generated sentence as a direct quote?

Only after checking its wording and attribution against the original audio. If you have only a summary, use an attributed paraphrase or obtain the source before quoting.

Are the timestamps exact edit boundaries?

No. Use reported times to locate candidate passages, then set your checked start and end in the original player or editor. Do not treat them as frame-exact cuts.

Do I need to review every second?

For a few selected quotes, check each selected passage with enough surrounding context. That verifies those passages only. A full published transcript needs a full review.

What if the audio is unclear?

Mark the line unclear, get a cleaner recording or clarification where possible, or omit it. Do not fill gaps with plausible words inferred from the picture.
