How to write a video creative brief from a reference ad

A reference ad is useful when it gives your team a shared target. A creative brief explains the decisions behind that target so you can make an original piece with the same strategic clarity.

Updated October 14, 2026 · 9 min read

Begin with the audience and the job to be done

Before describing colors or camera moves, state who the ad is for and what should change after they watch it. “People who need faster weekly reporting” is more useful than “busy professionals.” The job might be to understand a problem, believe a claim, or take a specific next step.

Brief essentials

  • Audience: who is most likely to recognize the problem?
  • Promise: what valuable outcome does the product offer?
  • Proof: what evidence makes the promise believable?
  • Objection: what concern could stop the viewer?
  • Action: what should the viewer do next?

Reverse-engineer the reference ad's structure

Write down what happens in order, then label the job each beat performs. A strong short ad often opens with a recognizable tension, makes the product visible, gives one concrete proof point, and closes with a clear action. The exact shots can change; the communication sequence is what your team needs to understand.

The timeline below is an illustrative editorial structure, not a report from an actual ad. Its ranges are proposed planning beats, not measured shot boundaries.

00:00–00:02
Pattern interrupt

Show the problem before the viewer has time to scroll.

00:02–00:08
Demonstration

Make the product's useful action visible, not merely described.

00:08–00:15
Proof and CTA

Give one reason to believe, then make the next step unmistakable.

Describe tone as behavior

“Make it premium” is hard to execute. Use observable choices: restrained editing, clean backgrounds, direct eye contact, a calm voiceover, and one accent color. Separate what the reference proves from what you are inferring. This keeps the brief confident without copying another brand's surface style.

Keep one message in the center

A brief becomes less useful every time it adds another audience, benefit, or CTA. Choose one primary promise. Supporting details should make that promise easier to believe, not compete with it. If the ad needs several products or offers, make the hierarchy explicit before production starts.

Use AI for observation, judgment for strategy

AI video analysis can propose observations about hooks, scenes, pacing, available dialogue, on-screen text, product moments, and calls to action. Check these against the source; brief actions and audio details can be missed. It cannot decide whether a claim is legally approved, whether the audience will trust it, or what your brand should stand for. Use the analysis as evidence for the brief, then let your strategist and creative team make the final choices.

Start with permission and a specific review question

Ask the client for a video they own or are permitted to share. Record which version you received and whether the permission covers private analysis, internal team use, or public republication. An ad appearing in a public library does not automatically give your team permission to reuse its footage, music or testimonials.

Choose one review question: does the opening name the problem, is the promised result demonstrated, or does the final action match the destination? A teardown can describe the communication choices visible in the clip. It cannot establish that an ad is profitable, winning, or expensive without campaign evidence.

Review the first three seconds without inventing a score

Play the opening at normal speed, then pause where needed. Record the opening promise, visible proof, transitions and audible beats. Use the actual generated report only for fields it contains. Add other checks as separately labelled human review. Leave speech unclear if you cannot hear it; a still image cannot verify a sound or camera move.

Opening review questions

  • What is literally shown or said?
  • What product or problem can the viewer identify?
  • What evidence is visible, and what remains a claim?
  • What would need a source replay or campaign data to confirm?

This checklist is an editorial review aid, not a numeric product score. TikTok's Creative Codes describe a hook, body and close structure. Google's ABCD guidance considers attention, branding, connection and direction. Use these as prompts for review, not a promise that one edit will improve conversions.

Separate evidence, edit hypothesis and production instruction

  1. Report observation: preserve an excerpt and candidate range from the real generated report. Verify it against the original before calling it confirmed.
  2. Editorial hypothesis: say what you would change and why. For example, “If the benefit is unclear, test an opening caption naming it.” This conditional suggestion is not an AI result or measured uplift.
  3. Production instruction: define the original subject, shot, message, duration and deliverable for the new piece. Use your own brand and authorized materials.

The handoff should make these distinctions easy to see. A director needs an executable instruction; a client needs to know which claims require approval. Avoid copying distinctive characters, a testimonial or a result claim just because it appears in the reference.
