How to analyze a video: build an evidence log, not just a recap

A useful video analysis makes it possible to retrace how a conclusion was reached. Record what is visible, what is audible, what is written on screen, and what remains your interpretation.

November 9, 2026 · 8 min read
The short method

Define the question first. Watch the entire source once. Then log important moments in chronological order with a timestamp, evidence type, direct observation, interpretation, and verification status. Replay each important claim before you share it. A polished summary without that trail can hide missed context.

Start with a question the video can answer

“Analyze this video” can mean many things. A marketer may need to check whether an ad demonstrates its promise. An editor may need a shot sequence. A trainer may need procedural steps. A researcher may need a claim timeline. Write the decision you need to make in one sentence, then include only observations relevant to it.

Keep the scope honest. A video can show a product being used, a line being spoken, or a scene changing. By itself, it usually cannot establish whether the campaign converted, why a person acted, or whether a displayed result is typical. Those require evidence outside the clip.

Use four evidence labels

Label the evidence before writing a conclusion
LabelWhat belongs hereExample wording
VisibleActions, objects, scene changes, composition, and text that can be read in the frame.“A blue container is placed beside the sink.”
AudibleDialogue, music, sound effects, or silence confirmed by listening to the source.“The speaker says ‘two minutes’; replay the audio to verify the number.”
On-screen textWords, numbers, labels, or captions shown in the video.“The end card displays ‘Save 20%’.”
InterpretationA human inference, proposed explanation, or editorial recommendation.“The close-up may be intended to make the product easier to recognize.”

Do not merge the labels. If a caption disagrees with the spoken line, preserve both and mark the conflict. If you cannot hear a word clearly, write “unclear” rather than reconstructing a plausible quote. W3C's media guidance distinguishes text transcripts of audio from descriptive transcripts that also convey important visual information.

Make a chronological evidence log

  1. Record the source version. Note the filename, version, duration, and whether the clip is complete or trimmed. Confirm you have the right to process and share it.
  2. Watch once without drafting. Note the beginning, middle, ending, and any major changes. This guards against treating the opening as the whole story.
  3. Add candidate moments. Use timestamps as navigation points. Write what appears or sounds at each moment before deciding what it means.
  4. Replay around important points. Include a little context before and after. Check quoted speech by listening; check displayed words by inspecting the image.
  5. Mark status and uncertainty. Use Not reviewed, Verified, Corrected, or Unclear. Keep a proposed edit separate from evidence in the source.

Next check: replay the source and read the label before describing its contents.

The sample is intentionally modest. It does not claim a frame-exact boundary, an audible beat, or a performance result. Those belong in the log only after the source or separate campaign data verifies them.

Choose the right level of analysis

  • Summary: main point, chronology, people or subjects, and key moments. See our video summary workflow.
  • Transcript check: verify exact spoken wording and speaker attribution against available audio. See the dialogue extraction guide.
  • Shot breakdown: document setup, framing, movement, lighting, and transitions for production planning. Use the shot list workflow.
  • Ad review: record the opening promise, visible proof, offer, and CTA, then separate creative observations from actual campaign outcomes. Start with video ad analysis when you have permission to submit the clip.

Automated tools can help organize a first pass, but they may omit a moment or misread speech, text, and intent. Treat generated timestamps as candidates. A frame sample or transcript is not proof that every frame or word was checked. Google documents video key moments as navigation aids, not as a substitute for checking the underlying video.

Questions to ask before sharing your analysis

How do I analyze a video step by step?

Define the question, review the whole source, record timestamped observations with evidence labels, separate interpretations, and replay important claims before sharing.

Can I analyze a video without a transcript?

Yes. You can log visible events and on-screen text. Mark dialogue unavailable or unverified, and do not infer exact speech from silent actions or captions alone.

Are AI video timestamps exact cut boundaries?

Use them to navigate to a candidate moment. Replay the original around the timestamp and mark an actual edit boundary yourself when precision matters.

What should a video analysis include?

Include the source version, analysis question, chronological moments, evidence type, observation, interpretation, uncertainty, and verification status.
